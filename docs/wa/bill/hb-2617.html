What the bill does (WA House Bill Analysis 2026 (Summary of Bill))

University of Washington: 60 percent Washington State University: 66 percent Eastern Washington University: 70 percent Central Washington University: 70 percent The Evergreen State College: 85 percent Western Washington University: 70 percent Community and technical colleges: 100 percent Every biennium thereafter, the amount of increases for employee compensation and central services funded by state funds must be increased by 10 percent until fully funded by state funds. If such compensation increases are funded with dedicated fund sources with insufficient revenue, additional funding from other sources is not provided. State funds are the NGF-O accounts, which include General Fund-State, the Education Legacy Trust Account, the Opportunity Pathways Account, and the Workforce Education Investment Account. Increases for compensation include increases set forth in employment contracts, cost of living adjustments, and legislatively approved collective bargaining agreements. Compensation is defined as the cost of salaries, wages, and pension contribution costs reported to the Office of Financial Management for purposes of the state budget and accounting systems with a funding source of an appropriated state fund or tuition. Employees are defined as any employee of a public institution of higher education, and include graduate teaching assistants. Employees do not include graduate research assistants performing research primarily related to their dissertation and who have incidental or no service expectations placed upon them by the institution. The Washington State Institute for Public Policy (WSIPP) must consult with public four-year institutions of higher education and the State Board for Community and Technical Colleges to conduct a study to define and evaluate essential higher education student services standards. The study must cover the following topics: academic support standards, including an evaluation of optimal student-to-advisor ratios, tutoring availability, writing center capacity, and library resource access necessary to maintain timely degree completion; wellness and basic needs infrastructure, including an assessment of the minimum necessary infrastructure for campus mental health services, including counselor-to-student ratios, and the effectiveness of benefits navigators in addressing student hunger and housing instability; instructional quality metrics, including a review of the impact of class sizes and the proportion of tenure-track faculty on student learning outcomes, undergraduate research opportunities, and faculty mentorship availability; career and workforce readiness, including an analysis of the services required to connect students with internships, undergraduate research, career counseling, and postgraduation employment in high-demand fields; and funding alignment, including a determination of the per-student cost required to provide these essential services and an analysis of how current funding levels, under the fund split methodology, have impacted the delivery of these services. Additionally, the WSIPP must solicit input from student associations, faculty organizations, staff unions, the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education, national research leaders in relevant areas, organizations providing community-based mentoring and advising services to first-generation and other underserved student populations, and community stakeholders to ensure the standards reflect the diverse needs of Washington's
