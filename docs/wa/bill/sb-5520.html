SB 5520 · Wrongly convicted persons

Broadens state compensation for wrongly convicted people by expanding actual innocence definitions, covering civil detention, and limiting attorney general challenges to claims.

What the bill does (WA Substitute Senate Bill Report (Summary of Bill))

Definitions. The definition of actually innocent describes a person where a preponderance of the all the evidence no longer supports a finding that the person engaged in conduct that is the basis for the felony. A definition is added for significant new exculpatory information that means any information, without regard to its admissibility, that supports or is consistent with a person's claim they were wrongly convicted and was not considered at trial or in a plea hearing. Filing a Claim—Preponderance of Evidence. Any person convicted in superior court and subsequently imprisoned for one or more felonies of which the person is actually innocent may file a claim for compensation from the state. An actionable claim must state facts and provide documentation in sufficient detail to determine, by a preponderance of the evidence, that: the claimant has been convicted of one or more felonies in superior court and subsequently sentenced to a term of imprisonment, and has served all or part of the sentence; the claimant did not engage in the conduct that is the basis for a felony charged in the charging document; the claimant did not commit or suborn perjury or fabricate evidence to cause or bring about the conviction; any form of guilty plea to a crime the claimant did not commit, or a confession that is false or illegally obtained, does not constitute perjury or fabricated evidence; the claimant is not currently incarcerated for any offense; and during the period of confinement or civil detention for which the claimant is seeking compensation, the claimant was not serving a term of imprisonment or a concurrent sentence for any crime other than the felony or felonies that are the basis for the claim. The claimant must state facts and present documentation in sufficient detail to determine by a preponderance of the evidence that: the claimant has received a pardon, commutation, or reprieve on grounds consistent with innocence; or the claimant's conviction was reversed or vacated following significant new exculpatory information, and: the claimant was not retried and the charges were dismissed; the claimant maintained their innocence and entered a plea pursuant to North Carolina v. Alford , 400 U.S. 25 (1970) or State v. Newton , 87 Wn.2d 363 (1976); or the claimant was retried and not convicted. The attorney general must concede the claimant was wrongly convicted if the claim meets the criteria for filing, except in the following circumstances where the attorney general may request an evidentiary hearing for the judge to assess the credibility or evidentiary value of the significant new exculpatory information in order to do further analysis to evaluate the claim: the conviction was reversed or vacated as part of postconviction proceedings based on the presentation of significant new exculpatory information, but no evidentiary hearing was held in court as part of the proceedings; or the significant new exculpatory information is a recantation, and there has not been a judicial finding of credibility. Claims under this chapter must be evaluated at all stages of the proceeding in a manner that promotes the remedial purpose of this chapter. Obtaining a Judgment—Clear and Convincing Evidence. All factors that were required for the filing of a claim must be proved by clear and convincing evidence.
